# What to do when a suspicious tech-support pop-up appears

**Editorial status:** Draft for UX, accessibility, bilingual, and technical review. Not yet published.

**Planned route:** `/suspicious-tech-support-pop-up.html`

**Audience:** A non-administrator who sees an alarming support message or receives technical-support contact that was not initiated through a trusted channel.

## Stop the interaction before deciding what it is

An alarming pop-up, unexpected call, message, or email may claim that your computer is infected, locked, or at immediate risk. It may provide a telephone number, request remote access, ask for a password or verification code, or demand urgent payment.

Do not follow those instructions while you decide whether the contact is legitimate. Do not call a number displayed in the warning, reply to the sender, open its links, make a payment, disclose a code, or grant remote access.

Use a support or security channel that you verify separately. If the device belongs to an organization, its policy and authorized response process take priority over this guide.

## Not every unusual support contact proves a scam

An unexpected or alarming message is a reason to pause, not proof by itself. Legitimate support may contact a user during an authorized case or scheduled service.

The important question is whether you can verify the contact independently:

- Did you initiate the support request through an approved channel?
- Does the case or appointment already exist?
- Can you reach the organization using contact information you already trust?
- Does the request follow the organization’s normal security and payment rules?

Do not use the telephone number, link, email address, or chat supplied by the suspicious contact to perform that verification.

## Warning signs that require you to stop

Stop the interaction and use a trusted channel when a prompt or contact:

- claims the device is infected or locked and tells you to call a displayed number;
- pressures you to act immediately or says you must keep the contact secret;
- asks for a password, verification code, recovery key, or unnecessary personal information;
- asks you to install remote-control software or approve remote access;
- demands payment by gift card, cryptocurrency, transfer, payment application, or an urgent support subscription;
- tells you to bypass security controls or organizational policy;
- claims to represent a known company but cannot be verified independently.

These signs help you decide to stop. They do not identify who created the message or prove the condition of the device.

## 1. Pause without following the instructions

Do not click buttons, links, telephone numbers, download prompts, or payment options in the suspicious content. Do not reply to the caller or sender to argue, test them, or request more information.

If no access, information, or payment has been shared, keep it that way. If something has already been shared, do not hide it or keep interacting in an attempt to reverse it yourself. Report exactly what happened to the authorized responder.

## 2. Leave the interaction without investigating it

If the browser tab or message can be closed through a normal, familiar control without accepting its instructions, close it. If it will not close, appears full-screen, repeatedly returns, or prevents normal use, stop trying different fixes and contact authorized support through another trusted route.

Do not reopen the content to collect evidence. Do not install a “cleaner,” “security tool,” browser extension, or remote-support application suggested by the warning.

Closing a window does not prove that the device is safe. It only ends the visible interaction when that can be done normally.

## 3. Use a separately verified support channel

Choose contact information that did not come from the suspicious prompt or sender. Depending on the device, this may be:

- the organization’s known help-desk portal or internal directory;
- a support address already provided by an employer or school;
- the official account or product support page reached independently;
- a trusted person responsible for the device or service.

If possible, use another trusted device to find or contact the authorized channel when the affected screen cannot be used normally. Do not assume that the first search result, advertisement, or caller is official.

## 4. Record only safe facts

Write down what you already saw or remember. Do not click, call back, reply, or reopen the content to obtain missing details.

Useful facts include:

- date and approximate time;
- whether it appeared as a browser pop-up, call, message, or email;
- the company or service the contact claimed to represent;
- the warning or request in your own words;
- the application or website you were using immediately before it appeared;
- actions you took before stopping;
- whether the device still responds normally.

A screenshot may help only if it can be captured without further interaction. Review it before sharing and remove passwords, codes, private messages, financial details, confidential documents, and unnecessary identifiers.

## 5. State what may already have been exposed

Tell the authorized responder clearly if any of the following occurred:

| Exposure area | What to report |
| --- | --- |
| Remote access | Whether remote-control software was installed, opened, or approved and whether another person controlled the device |
| Credentials | Whether a password, verification code, recovery key, or sign-in approval was provided |
| Personal information | What type of personal or organizational information was disclosed, without repeating it in an insecure message |
| Payment | Whether a payment, transfer, gift card, cryptocurrency transaction, or subscription was attempted or completed |
| Files or accounts | Whether the other person opened files, email, browser sessions, cloud storage, or account settings |

Do not send the password, code, financial number, or confidential content again when reporting the event. Describe the category and use the secure process chosen by the authorized responder.

## 6. Follow context-specific recovery instructions

The correct next action depends on what happened, who owns the device, which accounts were involved, and the organization’s incident process. Authorized support may provide separate instructions for device access, account protection, software review, payment concerns, or security reporting.

Do not apply a generic recovery sequence from this guide. In particular, do not:

- remove software, extensions, system files, or security tools without direction;
- disable networking, antivirus, encryption, or organizational controls as a universal response;
- reset a password on the possibly affected device unless the authorized process says it is safe;
- dispute a charge or attempt account recovery through contact details supplied by the suspicious sender;
- collect logs, trace the caller, or investigate who was responsible;
- assume that closing the pop-up means no further action is required.

## Prepare a concise report

Use this structure when contacting the trusted support or security channel:

### What appeared

Was it a pop-up, call, message, or email? What did it claim?

### When and where

When did it happen, and which application, website, account, or device was involved?

### What you did

Which links, buttons, downloads, calls, or approvals occurred before you stopped?

### What may have been shared

State whether remote access, credentials, personal information, payment, files, or accounts may be involved. Do not repeat sensitive values.

### Current state

Is the message still visible? Does the device respond normally? Has the suspicious contact tried again?

## What happens next

The authorized responder may ask follow-up questions and provide different instructions depending on the exposure. A complete, honest report is more useful than an attempted diagnosis or an incomplete account of what was shared.

## Scope note

This guide provides general recognition and reporting guidance. It does not determine whether a device is compromised, replace an organization’s security process, or provide malware removal, account recovery, payment-dispute, or incident-response instructions.
